## Where this interview sits

This is not a mathematics lecture. It is an interview from 18.06SC: Sarah Hansen asks, Gilbert Strang answers, and the subject is how he teaches linear algebra. It belongs at the front of the course because it explains the style of every lecture that follows — why Strang always draws the picture before writing the symbols, and why he stops at the board to let the room find the next step with him.

## The conversation's shape

Five questions organize the discussion:

1. What is your teaching system?
2. Why do you sometimes feign confusion, or genuinely get stuck?
3. What role does humor play?
4. How do you decide when to go into detail?
5. How do you connect with a large lecture hall full of people at different levels?

His answers reduce to one instruction: **do not do the thinking for the student**.

## The central claims

### No system, just thinking alongside them

Asked for his system, Strang says he has none. What he offers instead is a stance:

- he likes students and wants to help;
- the key is to **think the question through again with them**, not to announce the answer;
- thinking takes time, so a proof cannot be zipped through.

This also explains the "feigned confusion" rumor. He does pause at critical points, and the pause is not a performance: the next step is genuinely worth a moment.

### Symbols, meaning, reason — in three passes

Strang describes an order:

1. put the symbols on the board;
2. ask what they mean;
3. only then ask why the statement is true.

Demanding a proof first assumes an intuition that most listeners have not built yet. Let the intuition stand up, and the proof has somewhere to land.

> **One-sentence summary:** Teaching is not saying the correct thing quickly; it is giving the listener a chance to arrive at the correct place themselves.

### Willing to get stuck in public

The interviewer asks whether thinking live in front of hundreds of people is risky. He admits it is, and that he has lost the thread or hit dead ends. He thinks that is fine:

- students will hit dead ends anyway;
- watching a teacher climb out of one is a transferable lesson;
- a teacher who is always fluent only makes students feel inadequate.

The point is to make the **process** of solving, not the result, the content of the class.

### Humor humanizes the subject

Strang ties humor to humanizing mathematics. A book is not a person, but a person wrote it; a pun — "keep things in their Gauss-given order" — signals that the symbols in front of you were produced by someone who could also make a joke.

Humor is not decoration here. It reduces distance.

### How deep to go: are you actually rethinking it?

His criterion is simple: **if he is not thinking it through himself, he is probably going too fast**. When he re-derives the step, the right words and the right sequence appear on their own; reciting from memory breaks the connection with the room.

Connecting with a large class does not mean reaching every person. It means staying **conscious of where the audience is**, rather than staring at an operation like "A inverse".

## Actionable conclusions

For a learner, three practices follow:

1. **Allow the pause.** When a step does not land, go back to the first layer — symbols, then meaning, then reason.
2. **Recite the process, not the answer.** Close the book and explain to yourself why a step holds. Where the explanation breaks is where the understanding is missing.
3. **Accept being stuck.** Being stuck is part of learning; the skill to practice is the move that gets you out of the corner.

For a teacher, a short checklist:

- Am I doing the audience's thinking for them?
- Did I leave time for "what is the next step?"
- Am I teaching the result, or the road to it?
