## A toy task that runs through the lecture

**Number Search**: the environment holds an integer from 1 to 16 and the agent gets four guesses. After each guess the environment says "higher", "lower", or "correct". Correct earns reward 1; running out of guesses earns 0.

The optimal first guess is 8, because it halves the candidate set — the same logic as an opening guess in Wordle. Starting at 4 can still succeed by following feedback; starting at 16, or counting up from 1, is likely to time out.

The task is simple enough to compute trajectory probabilities by hand, yet it contains multi-step decisions, sparse reward, and partial observability. Almost every RL concept can be demonstrated in it.

## Three structural failures of SFT

Supervised fine-tuning maximizes the probability of the demonstrated action given the true history:

$$
J_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = \sum_{t} \log \pi_\theta(a_t^* \mid h_t)
$$

That is not the same objective as making the task succeed.

### 1. Task mismatch

- Tasks usually have several valid paths: 8 is the best first guess, but 4 is workable. A demonstration records only one.
- Worse, **every action that was not demonstrated is treated as equally bad**. After "higher", guessing 7 and guessing 15 are both wrong, but one is close and the other wildly off. The SFT loss cannot see the difference.

### 2. Data mismatch

Failed trajectories carry information — they say "do not do this". But SFT would raise the probability of exactly those actions, so failed data cannot be used directly.

### 3. Exposure bias

At training time the history comes from demonstrations; at inference it comes from the model. Once the model leaves the training distribution it has never seen such a state, and its behavior may be badly calibrated. It also never learns **to recover from its own mistakes**.

RL addresses all three: it optimizes the task return directly, can learn from failed trajectories, and trains on data generated by the current policy (on-policy).

## Writing interaction as a trajectory

- Policy $\pi_\theta$: the action distribution parameterized by the language model.
- Task instance: the hidden number, invisible to the agent.
- Observation: what the environment returns ("higher").
- Action: the next guess.
- Reward: 1 on success, 0 on timeout.

A trajectory $\tau$ interleaves actions and observations, and $h_t$ is the history up to time $t$.

The trajectory probability factors into an environment term and a policy term:

$$
p_\theta(\tau) = \prod_t p_{\text{env}}(o_t, r_t \mid h_t, a_t)\, \pi_\theta(a_t \mid h_t)
$$

The key point: **we never need to know the environment term**. Interacting with the environment samples from it. That is also why RL is expensive — it obtains information about the gradient only indirectly, by trying things.

> Background: this uses the partially observable MDP formulation, because the agent cannot see the true state and must condition on history.

## Objective: maximize expected return

$$
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[R(\tau)\right]
$$

The lecture's simplified illustration has four possible trajectories with probabilities 0.3, 0.2, 0.25, and 0.25, two of which succeed. Expected return is 0.5, and training should move probability mass from the failures to the successes.

## The simplest method: expert iteration and REST

Before policy gradients, there is a baseline that needs almost no new math: **Reinforced Self-Training (REST)**.

1. Sample a batch of trajectories from the current policy.
2. Compute rewards.
3. Keep only successful trajectories (a threshold filter, trivial with binary reward).
4. Run ordinary SFT on them.
5. Repeat, adding newly sampled successes to a growing training set.

It reuses the entire SFT stack and is really self-training plus reward filtering. It is not RL in the strict sense, but it already has the core structure: data comes from the model, and the update direction comes from reward.

A practical problem: if the model cannot sample a single success, neither REST nor RL has anything to work with. Remedies include a small demonstration cold start, curriculum tasks the model can already solve, intermediate rewards, or reward shaping that pays for valuable sub-steps.

## Policy gradients: REINFORCE

Differentiating $J(\theta)$ directly runs into two obstacles:

1. Actions are **sampled**, and sampling is not differentiable.
2. The environment is a black box, almost always non-differentiable.

The classic solution is REINFORCE (Williams, 1992). Differentiate the expectation and apply the score-function identity:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
= \sum_\tau \nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau)
= \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[R(\tau)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)\right]
$$

The log-probability of a trajectory decomposes into per-step action terms, and the environment term vanishes because it does not depend on $\theta$:

$$
\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)
= \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid h_t)
$$

### What the implementation looks like

1. Sample one trajectory.
2. Compute log-probabilities only for **tokens the model generated** (tool output and prompt tokens are excluded).
3. Multiply every token log-probability from that trajectory by the same scalar reward.
4. Backpropagate.

The lecture's observation is striking: this looks almost exactly like the SFT gradient, with a reward multiplied in front. One action may span several tokens; their log-probabilities are summed.

## Why plain REINFORCE is not enough

Consider a successful trajectory and a failed one.

- Every action in the successful trajectory is upweighted by reward 1.
- The failed trajectory has reward 0, and **multiplying by zero produces no update at all**.

But failures are exactly what we want to learn from. The update should be relative to how well the policy was expected to do:

- On an easy task the model solves 95% of the time, a rare careless failure should be penalized heavily.
- On a genuinely hard task, failure says little about how bad the action was.

This is the **advantage**:

$$
A(h_t, a_t) = Q^\pi(h_t, a_t) - V^\pi(h_t)
$$

It measures how much better an action is than the policy's average behavior.

### A concrete advantage example

After guessing 8 ("higher") and 12 ("lower"), the answer is 9, 10, or 11.

- Guess 11: if wrong, only one guess and two candidates remain. Advantage is low.
- Guess 10: correct or not, the range still narrows. Advantage is high.

## Baselines: same expectation, less variance

Subtract a baseline $b(h_t)$ that depends only on history:

$$
\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\left[(R(\tau) - b(h_t))\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid h_t)\right]
$$

Because $\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid h)] = 0$, subtracting the baseline adds **no bias**; it only changes variance.

### The two-action example

A policy with one parameter $\theta$ uses a sigmoid to produce the probability of action A. At the current $\theta$, $p(A)=0.25$, A has reward 1, and B has reward 0.

The true gradient is $p(1-p)=0.1875$.

- Without a baseline: sampling A gives $1 \times 0.75 = 0.75$; sampling B gives $0 \times (-0.25) = 0$. The estimate jumps between 0.75 and 0.
- With baseline 0.5: sampling A gives $(1-0.5)\times 0.75 = 0.375$; sampling B gives $(0-0.5)\times(-0.25) = 0.125$. The expectation is still 0.1875, but both samples sit closer to it.

A baseline can be a constant, a running average of rewards, or a separately trained model (actor-critic, PPO). GRPO's innovation is to avoid the extra model entirely and estimate the baseline from multiple rollouts of the same task.

> If rewards arrive at intermediate steps rather than only at the end, replacing the reward with reward-to-go further reduces variance.

## GRPO: the group mean as baseline

Group Relative Policy Optimization:

1. Sample a group of trajectories for one task instance (typically around eight).
2. Compute each trajectory's reward.
3. Use the group mean as the baseline:

$$
A_i = R_i - \frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G} R_j
$$

4. Scale each trajectory's token log-probabilities by its advantage and take the policy gradient step.

Because the baseline depends only on the prompt and the model, the estimate stays unbiased. Group comparison happens within one task, so difficulty is never mixed across tasks.

### Two corrections from DR-GRPO

- **Do not divide by the group standard deviation.** Dividing standardizes across difficulties and strips the advantage of any sense of task difficulty. Subtract the mean only.
- **Do not divide by generation length.** Normalizing by token count favors long wrong answers. Divide by a constant — the maximum possible generation length.

The original GRPO also includes importance-ratio clipping and a KL penalty against the initial policy for stability; those come in the next lecture.

### All-correct or all-wrong groups carry no signal

If every trajectory fails, the task is too hard for the current policy. If every trajectory succeeds, it is too easy. Either way group variance is zero, all advantages are zero, and nothing is learned. Remedies: different tasks, hints, demonstrations, an SFT cold start, or reward shaping.

## Three methods, one family

| Method | How sampled trajectories are weighted |
| --- | --- |
| REST | Keep successes only, then SFT |
| REINFORCE | Scale log-probabilities by trajectory reward |
| GRPO | Scale log-probabilities by group-relative advantage |

All three learn from the model's own trajectories; they differ only in **how each action is reweighted**. All three address the three SFT failures: optimize return directly, learn from failures, and keep training and inference distributions aligned.

The cost is real interaction with the environment: slow, expensive, and noisy in the gradient. The next two lectures cover stabilization and systems.

## Takeaways

- SFT optimizes "look like the demonstration"; RL optimizes "earn the return".
- Policy gradients multiply the log-probability gradient by reward, sidestepping sampling and the environment.
- Plain REINFORCE gives no signal on zero-reward trajectories; advantages make updates relative to expected performance.
- A history-only baseline adds no bias and reduces variance.
- GRPO uses the mean of multiple rollouts of the same task as its baseline, avoiding a separate value model.
- A group with no variation in success or failure yields no learning signal.
