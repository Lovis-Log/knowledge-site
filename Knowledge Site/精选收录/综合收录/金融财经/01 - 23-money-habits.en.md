## What this checklist is for

Humphrey Yang, a former financial advisor, compresses personal finance into 23 concrete actions ordered roughly as: measure where you stand, control cash flow, handle credit, configure accounts, then adjust behaviour. It works best as a self-audit: go through the list and check what you actually do, rather than treating it as 23 disconnected tips.

The thread running through the video is that **much of the value comes from the order, not from knowing the item**. The same dollar does very different work in an emergency fund versus a brokerage account, and the same hour spent optimizing a $50,000 portfolio versus negotiating a raise returns ten times less.

## Step one: measure where you stand

**Net worth.** Assets minus liabilities. The video's example: a $100,000 house, $25,000 in accounts, and a $30,000 car loan give 100,000 + 25,000 − 30,000 = $95,000. Note that the house counts at its equity value, not its listing price.

The benchmark is not other people's absolute numbers but the median for your age. Yang suggests aiming for double that median: the median for people in their thirties is about $34,691, so the baseline is roughly $70,000.

Update every three to six months. The reason is worth keeping: if the portfolio is part of the number, month-to-month swings mostly reflect the market rather than your behaviour, so monthly tracking turns noise into apparent signal.

**Roth IRA.** The 2026 limit is $7,500 a year, provided you have earned income. The difference from a traditional IRA is the timing of tax: Roth contributions come from after-tax money, and both contributions and growth come out tax-free in retirement; a traditional IRA is funded pre-tax and taxed on withdrawal.

The compounding difference is easiest to feel with the video's numbers: a lifetime of contributions ending at a $2.2 million balance on roughly $300,000 of contributions means about $1.9 million of gains that are never taxed. In a taxable account, that $1.9 million would be taxed at withdrawal.

**Emergency fund.** The baseline is three months of essential expenses — food, rent, insurance, minimum debt payments — excluding discretionary spending. At $5,000 a month of essentials, that is $15,000.

Yang then adds months for specific circumstances:

- Single-income household: +1 month, because there is no second paycheque as a backstop
- Variable income (commission, tips, freelancing): +1 month
- Dependants (children, a parent you support): +1 month
- Owning your home: +1 month, because repairs are sudden and expensive

A single-income parent who owns a home hits three of the four conditions, which means six months — $30,000 at $5,000 of monthly essentials, close to double what a dual-income renter without children needs.

**High-yield savings.** Keep the emergency fund and idle cash in a high-yield savings account rather than checking. Yang quotes roughly 3%–3.5%: $10,000 earns about $350 more a year. Checking only needs enough to cover the month's bills.

## Step two: three rules for spending decisions

**Affordable is not the same as wise.** A common guideline is rent at up to 35% of income, but if a perfectly good apartment costs 25%, stretching to 35% just to use the budget is backwards. The replacement test: does the spending match your priorities, and will it become a monthly obligation you have to budget around?

**Renegotiate recurring bills.** Internet, phone, software and insurance can be repriced with one phone call a year. Yang calls Comcast annually, mentions he is considering other providers, and the retention team typically renews the introductory offer for another year or two. For car insurance, comparison tools can save $100 or more a month on their own.

**Price large purchases per hour.** For a first-class seat you pay for yourself, divide the fare by the flight hours: $5,000 for ten hours is $500 an hour for the seat. That can be rational at a high income or net worth, but for younger travellers the money usually does more in the trip itself or in the market. Three exceptions the video allows: points or miles, a status upgrade, or someone else paying.

**Invest in your income, not portfolio optimization.** This is the most counterintuitive item. On a $50,000 portfolio, one extra percentage point of return is $500 a year; a job change, promotion or salary negotiation is often $5,000–10,000 a year, more than ten times as much. A raise also compounds, because it lifts the baseline for the next ten, twenty or thirty years. Yang's threshold: below roughly $250,000–500,000 in investable assets, optimizing the portfolio is rarely the best use of your time.

## Step three: credit as an invisible asset

**Utilization.** Balances divided by total limits, kept under 30% and ideally under 10%. Two cards with a combined $10,000 limit and $2,000 owed is 20%. Lenders read anything above 30% as risk, and the score drops.

**Never carry a balance.** Yang pays his cards every few days. Paying in full means using the grace period and rewards rather than being eaten by interest. His addendum is blunt: if you cannot be disciplined with a card, do not get one.

**Scores and pulls.** Checking your own score is a soft pull and does not affect it. Applying for a car loan, mortgage or new card triggers a hard pull, costs roughly 5–10 points, and stays on the report for two years — so avoid several in a short window.

## Step four: accounts, fees and ordering

**Know what "enough" means.** Money easily becomes a scoreboard, and comparing with peers never ends. The FIRE convention is 25 times annual spending: $50,000 a year implies about $1.25 million. Past that point the marginal gain in lifestyle is small.

**The 401(k) match.** Employers often match dollar for dollar, for example up to $3,000 a year. Contributing $3,000 and receiving $3,000 is an immediate doubling — the highest return on the list.

**Fees.** Actively managed mutual funds average roughly 0.5%–0.75%; passive index funds are usually under 0.2%, and Vanguard's S&P 500 fund is close to 0.03%. Over decades, a half-point difference compounds into a large sum.

**Backdoor Roth.** In 2026, single filers earning above $168,000 and married couples above $252,000 cannot contribute directly to a Roth IRA. The workaround: contribute to a traditional IRA (no income limit), leave it uninvested, then convert it to a Roth inside the brokerage app. The complication is the pro rata rule — if you already hold a traditional IRA with invested funds, part of the conversion becomes taxable.

**HSA.** A health savings account has three tax benefits: contributions are deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. Yang's priority order is emergency fund, then the 401(k) match, then the Roth IRA, then the HSA.

**The children's account.** The video describes a policy giving US citizen children born between 1 January 2025 and 31 December 2028 a one-time $1,000 federal deposit into a dedicated account invested in low-cost US equity index funds (fees capped at 0.1%). The money is locked until the child turns 18, and gains are taxed as ordinary income on withdrawal. Yang's verdict: less efficient than a Roth, but free money is free money. Note that this is a US policy described in the video; eligibility and tax treatment should be confirmed against official sources.

## Step five: behaviour and relationships

**Automate savings.** Move money to savings on payday. Yang cites a study tracking 400 grocery shoppers: those with larger account balances consistently overspent even when they arrived with a plan. A healthy balance creates a sense of abundance that invites spending, so automating at least puts a barrier between you and the cash you can see.

**Diversify.** Buffett's line, quoted in the video: diversification is protection against ignorance. Fewer than 5–10 holdings, or heavy concentration in one asset class, is worth revisiting; someone who does not follow the market should simply hold an index fund covering 500 to thousands of companies. Those who do follow it can keep a limited sleeve of high-conviction positions.

**Do not lend to friends and family.** Once a loan goes unpaid, the relationship changes shape. The substitute: if someone asks for $2,000 and you want to help, give $200 outright and say it does not need to be repaid and should not be asked for again. You lose a little money and avoid years of awkwardness.

**Talk about money.** Discussing income, investments and spending is still taboo, but saying it out loud is one of the fastest ways to improve at it.

**A fun-money account.** For people wired for extreme frugality: ring-fence a small amount as guilt-free spending. The argument is that you cannot take it with you, and total self-denial is hard to sustain.

## Closing: estate planning

The last item is the one young people skip. If anyone depends on you, or you have assets you want distributed as you choose, you should have an estate plan. The core documents are a living will and a healthcare power of attorney, recording your preferences if something happens. Yang's own experience settling his father's estate without a trust convinced him to recommend going straight to a trust attorney.

## Comprehension check

1. **A house worth $600,000 with a $350,000 mortgage, $40,000 in savings and a $20,000 car loan — what is the net worth?**
   Answer: Home equity is 600 − 350 = $250,000; add $40,000 and subtract $20,000, giving $270,000.

2. **Essentials cost $4,000 a month, single income, renting, no children. What is the emergency-fund target?**
   Answer: Three months of baseline is $12,000; one extra month for single income makes four months, or $16,000.

3. **Why does "invest in your income" usually beat "optimize the portfolio"? Give the video's order of magnitude.**
   Answer: The base is too small when the portfolio is small. One extra point on $50,000 is $500 a year, while a raise of $5,000–10,000 is more than ten times that and lifts the baseline for the rest of a career. The crossover is around $250,000–500,000 in investable assets.

4. **Total credit limit $8,000 with $1,600 owed — what is the utilization, and where does it sit?**
   Answer: 20%, below the 30% danger line but above the 10% ideal.

5. **What is the core difference between a Roth IRA and a traditional IRA?**
   Answer: The timing of tax. Roth uses after-tax money and is fully tax-free on withdrawal in retirement; traditional contributions are pre-tax and taxed as ordinary income when withdrawn.

6. **Who needs a backdoor Roth?**
   Answer: Anyone earning above the Roth IRA limits ($168,000 single, $252,000 married in 2026). The method is to contribute to a traditional IRA and convert it without investing, watching the pro rata rule.
