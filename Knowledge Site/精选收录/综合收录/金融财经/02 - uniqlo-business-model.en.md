## Why this company is worth unpacking

Uniqlo is the world's third-largest fashion retailer. The business looks suspiciously simple: a handful of colours, a few timeless silhouettes, better materials than competitors, and bulk orders large enough to keep prices fair. What actually holds that surface up is three things — fabric science developed with Toray, a global supply chain that runs almost like machinery, and a brand strategy built on borrowing credibility from the very best partners.

Bell's framing is that Uniqlo is not a clothing company but a technology company: it treats "basic, durable, fairly priced" as an engineering problem.

## From a menswear shop to LifeWear

In 1984 Tadashi Yanai opened a unisex casual-wear shop in Hiroshima called Unique Clothing Warehouse. When he registered the brand in Hong Kong in 1988, the letter C was mistyped as a Q — and Yanai preferred the look of the misspelling. Uniqlo, as we know it, was born from a typo.

Before that, he grew up above his father's small tailoring shop in Ube, Japan. After university he returned to the shop, starting with sweeping floors and selling suits, and eventually inherited it. He travelled to the United States to study Gap, which directly inspired the original store and much of the later strategy.

Two influences shaped the guiding principle. The first was Gap's model of scaling basics. The second was Peter Drucker's teaching that a company should start from what the customer wants rather than what the business wants to sell. Together they became LifeWear: abandon trend-chasing and perfect everyday basics instead. Every decision since can be tested against that principle.

## Three SPA models: speed, forecast, engineering

Uniqlo, Zara and H&M all run a SPA model — specialty retailer of private-label apparel — meaning they design, produce, sell and ship their own clothes without middlemen. That differs from Levi's, which sells both through its own stores and through Target, Nordstrom and Amazon.

The difference is cadence:

- **Zara is the reactive one.** It chases runway and social trends and gets designs into stores within weeks.
- **H&M is forecast-driven.** It follows trends too, but basics are a large part of the mix, so it predicts the product mix far in advance and orders enormous quantities to drive unit costs down.
- **Uniqlo is engineered.** It invests in fabric technology and R&D before a garment is stitched, often years ahead.

Bell calls the third an "engineered approach" — not a phrase usually associated with fashion retail, and precisely the point of difference.

## Toray: treating fabric as technology

Making that approach real depends on one long-standing partnership: Toray Industries, a Japanese textile giant that does not make garments of this kind. Toray specialises in advanced carbon-fibre composites and performance chemicals — the materials found in aircraft and golf clubs. For a basics brand, that is an unusual partner to choose.

Through that partnership Uniqlo produced its best-selling proprietary lines, several with trademarked scientific components behind them — HeatTech and AIRism among them.

## Four defining products

**The 1998 fleece jacket.** Bell dates modern Uniqlo from here, even though it is not the most famous item. Fleece had been a luxury textile in the 1980s; a market downturn made buying it in bulk cheap. Uniqlo bet on it, sourcing large volumes of polyester fibre from Toray, and sold the jacket for $20 when similar products elsewhere cost about $100. Sales went from 2 million units in 1998 to 8.5 million in 1999 and 26 million in 2000. That bet made the brand a household name.

**HeatTech in 2003.** A thin thermal innerwear line developed with Toray. It traps body moisture in micro air pockets in the fabric and converts it into heat. The line has sold more than a billion units worldwide and is the clearest example of Uniqlo's "technology you cannot see".

**Ultra Light Down in 2009.** A down jacket that packs into its own pocket and emphasises an extreme warmth-to-weight ratio.

**AIRism in 2013.** A fine polyester fabric designed to feel like a second skin, including in hot and humid climates.

What the four share is that none is a design breakthrough. Each is a materials and structure breakthrough, then pushed to enormous volume.

## Why it stays affordable

High-end fabric plus R&D, yet the price stays reasonable, because cost is squeezed from both ends.

**Fewer styles.** Zara orders in small batches because it pivots quickly; Uniqlo does the opposite. It sells timeless basics with a narrow range, so it can order enormous quantities of a few colours rather than small quantities of many. Concentrated volume earns purchasing discounts and keeps production lines stable.

**Where and how it is made.** Uniqlo does not own factories, but it holds multi-year relationships with a small network in low-cost countries: China, Vietnam, Bangladesh and Indonesia. Quality control is not left to chance. Since 2000 Uniqlo has stationed a team of Japanese master craftsmen — the Takumi team — inside partner factories to give technical instruction from thread to final stitch. Manufacturing cost stays low while someone is still on the floor signing off on the process.

## Scale and real-time data

Revenue was $11.2 billion (1.77 trillion yen) in 2021 and $18.6 billion in 2025, a 66% increase. Store count grew only 9% over the same period, so the growth came from higher sales per store, e-commerce and flagship performance rather than from opening stores.

The mix shifted too. Japan is now a mature market; it used to be about half of revenue, while international sales are now two-thirds. Growth therefore comes mainly from abroad.

Scale brings a specific risk: ordering in millions of units and forecasting months to a year ahead means a wrong call leaves warehouses full of unsold clothes.

The answer was the 2017 Ariake project, which moved design, R&D and logistics teams onto one floor of the Tokyo headquarters, above the largest warehouse, explicitly to close the gap between what is selling and what gets restocked.

The supporting technology is RFID. Every garment carries a chip that scans at self-checkout, sending point-of-sale data back to headquarters immediately. Headquarters can therefore watch which items move and which stall, then speed up or slow down year-round factory lines accordingly. For a company of this size, that loop from real-time point-of-sale data to production schedule is genuinely rare.

## Who buys it, and why it reads as "cool"

The core customer is 25 to 45, lives in or near a city, and wants good basics at a fair price. Concretely, that can be a student buying a $19 T-shirt because it fits the budget, or someone earning several hundred thousand dollars who wants no visible logos and basics that last. Serving both ends of the income range is unusual at this price point.

That reach produces a rare position: cheap and cool at the same time. As quiet luxury has grown, Uniqlo — despite decidedly unluxury prices — supplies exactly the anonymous blank canvas that quiet luxury describes: simple silhouettes, interchangeable pieces, a workable capsule wardrobe.

The more deliberate move is borrowing credibility. Bell lists collaborations that look unrelated on paper: an ongoing collection with JW Anderson, creative director of Dior and formerly of Loewe, running almost a decade; a global creative director, Clare Waight Keller, previously creative director at Chloé and Givenchy; a $300 million, ten-year endorsement with Roger Federer in 2018 to weigh in on design and represent the brand; and a global ambassadorship with Cate Blanchett.

The through-line is **mastery**. Uniqlo partners with people and companies that are the best at what they do, borrows part of that credibility, and never passes the cost of it into the price.

## Is it actually fast fashion

Bell notes that 67% of viewers think of Uniqlo as fast fashion, and argues the answer is not binary.

By definition, fast fashion produces clothing quickly and cheaply in response to the latest trends. Measured against that:

- **Not fast.** Uniqlo plans products up to a year ahead and does not chase the newest trends. It can stay nimble within that year using sales data, but it is clearly not in Zara's — let alone Shein's — weeks-long response cycle.
- **Cheap to make, yes.** It manufactures exclusively in low-cost labour markets and at mass volume, two core features of fast fashion.

So Uniqlo sits in a grey area: **fast-fashion style manufacturing applied to an anti-fast-fashion product philosophy**. The two can coexist. It does not escape every feature of fast fashion, but material quality and brand reputation keep customers returning.

## Two variables for the future

**The opportunity is international expansion.** Since recent growth came from outside Japan, North America is the focus. The brand has said it aims to nearly triple its North American store count between 2024 and the end of 2027, and Bell sees US penetration as still low — the largest runway for growth.

**The risk is succession.** Tadashi Yanai has steered the company since 1984 and remains deeply involved in daily decisions, but he is in his late seventies. The most likely successor is the current president and COO, who has led the North American expansion. The candidate looks strong, but a leadership change at this scale is always a risk.

Bell's conclusion: Uniqlo holds high-quality science-backed clothing, affordable prices and a genuinely cool reputation at the same time — a combination competitors would pay dearly to have.

## Comprehension check

1. **All three retailers use the SPA model. What separates them?**
   Answer: Zara responds to trends within weeks; H&M forecasts the mix far ahead and uses huge orders to lower unit cost; Uniqlo engineers the fabric first and plans products up to a year ahead.

2. **Why could the 1998 fleece jacket sell for $20?**
   Answer: Fleece had been a luxury textile, and a market downturn made bulk purchasing cheap. Uniqlo bought polyester fibre in large volume from Toray, and the jacket sold for $20 against roughly $100 elsewhere. Units went from 2 million to 26 million between 1998 and 2000.

3. **How does HeatTech work?**
   Answer: It traps body moisture in micro air pockets in the fabric and converts it into heat. The line has sold over a billion units.

4. **Revenue grew 66% while stores grew 9% — what does that imply?**
   Answer: Growth came from productivity per store, e-commerce and flagships rather than expansion, with international sales rising from about half to two-thirds of revenue as Japan matured.

5. **What specific problem do the Ariake project and RFID solve?**
   Answer: The inventory risk of forecasting far ahead at massive volume. Co-locating design, R&D and logistics shortens the information loop; RFID returns real-time sales data at checkout so factory lines can be sped up or slowed down.

6. **Why is the fast-fashion label a grey area for Uniqlo?**
   Answer: Its product philosophy is anti-fast-fashion — planned a year ahead, not trend-chasing, built to last — while its manufacturing is fast-fashion style: low-cost countries and mass volume.

7. **What is the common thread across Toray, JW Anderson, Federer and Blanchett?**
   Answer: Mastery. Each is the best in their field, and Uniqlo borrows their credibility without passing the cost into its prices.
