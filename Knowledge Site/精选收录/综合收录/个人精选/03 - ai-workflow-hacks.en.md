## What this material addresses

Dan Martell builds AI products every day, and here he answers the questions he is asked most. The opening image sets up the problem: **using AI as a fancy search engine is like hiring a private chef and only ever ordering a peanut butter sandwich.**

His answers are less about which tool to pick and more about processes you can copy: supply enough context, use a structured prompt to get specific output, then turn the repetitive parts into skills or automations. Tool lists change; the process does not.

## 1. Give enough context: talking beats typing

The first question is how to give AI the context it needs without typing pages of text.

His principle is to **dictate** — he talks about three times faster than he types, so across chat tools, coding platforms and agents he speaks and lets transcription do the work, which also accumulates context over time.

The second trick hands initiative to the model: end a prompt with "ask me questions if you need more context". Letting the AI pull information beats guessing what it needs, and multiple-choice or checklist questions are easier to answer than one long description.

## 2. Escaping generic answers: MAPS

AI is trained on everything humanity has produced, so by default it condenses and averages the best answer — which is why unconstrained output feels generic.

His framework is MAPS:

- **M — Mission:** the goal behind the task. Not "write copy" but "help me get more customers".
- **A — Ask:** specifically what to produce, such as a call list of leads or a landing page to capture emails. Most people supply only this layer, which is why answers stay vague.
- **P — Parameters:** everything about your situation — past client information, scripts that already worked, product documents. He notes you can hand over two or three full books of material, while most people give a paragraph or two.
- **S — Shape:** the form of the output — spreadsheet, bulleted list, or a short answer.

When documents are scarce, dictate instead: talk through everything you know. He also suggests a service that answers with several models side by side, effectively convening a small review panel.

## 3. Letting AI know you: the master prompt

The familiar annoyance is that every new chat starts from zero, like talking to a stranger.

His approach moves from answering questions to letting the tool look for itself:

1. Connect the tools where your information already lives — email, cloud drive, notes, Slack.
2. Ask it to build the most detailed master prompt about who you are, what you are working on and who is in your life; correct the parts it got wrong.
3. Save that document into the AI's settings, or paste it into your other AI tools.

Every new chat then starts with context, and the answers stop reading like generic advice. He adds a trust consideration: if financial information and internal documents are going in, whether the vendor treats your data as payment for a "free" product is part of the decision.

## 4. Learning and goals

**Learning faster.** His target is to learn any topic in under 30 minutes, roughly 20 times faster. The steps: have it find the best **recent** resources on the topic — old material is not useful in fast-moving fields — then build a learning plan from those and teach it to you. That skips irrelevant chapters so you learn only the part you need. He names NotebookLM as his daily tool: it searches resources, builds a chat-able study set, and outputs audio, presentations and quizzes.

**Five-year goals.** A goal you cannot see is a goal you cannot hit, and most goals are too vague ("make more money", "get healthier"). Three steps: ask it to rewrite the goal as SMART (specific, measurable, attainable, realistic, timely); answer its follow-up questions out loud with dictation; then ask it to describe what life looks like when the goal is hit — sights, sounds, details, feelings — and turn that into an image. One practical detail: re-render it at phone-wallpaper proportions so the goal is in front of you a hundred times a day.

## 5. Execution and consistency

**Systems instead of willpower.** Three steps:

1. Give the AI the goals.
2. Have it break them into a written plan with timelines.
3. Have it build a daily dashboard: list the activities that must happen today, enter what you actually did, and let it compute progress against the time remaining using red, yellow and green — red below 10% of the required activity, yellow within 10% of it, green on track.

His observation is blunt: if the information is ugly and confusing, nobody will open it daily; make it pretty and gamified and you will. The advanced version turns the dashboard into a mobile app or a web page saved to the home screen.

**Calendar.** His premise: goals are not things you want, they are the by-product of where your time goes, which is why everything in his life is in his calendar so he can audit it. The method is to connect the calendar and give it this quarter's goals, then instruct it to review the calendar like a ruthless mentor — argue about what needs restructuring, say why, and optimise for the goals rather than what he wants right now.

He estimates most founders have about ten hours a week in their calendar on things they do not need to be doing.

## 6. Output that sounds like you: email

AI-written email has a recognisable texture. The fix is to let it learn your corpus: connect the mailbox, have it analyse how you write, and produce a tone document describing how you write to your boss, your friends and your clients differently. Let that document steady every draft, and refresh it with new messages periodically.

## 7. Handing repetitive work to AI

His rule is borrowed from programming: **DRY — don't repeat yourself**. The second time you do something, stop and turn it into an automation.

The process: record your screen while narrating what you are doing and why; hand the recording to the AI to convert into a reusable skill; then schedule that skill to run.

The difference between tools lies in how they take over. Some seize the whole screen and make you sit and watch; others capture the process, run it in the background and save it as a skill, so you can set it and forget it.

## 8. Information and finances

**Scattered notes.** Three steps: choose one home database and consolidate everything into it; point your other AI tools at it; then let an agent tidy it overnight — deleting what is not needed, connecting what should be connected, filing things where other agents can reach them. He keeps a personal hub in Obsidian while his team uses Notion.

**Financials.** The obstacle is not the tool but the data: most accounting software is not AI-friendly, and most operators do not want to organise their numbers, so garbage in, garbage out. The fix is to connect bank statements and payment systems to the AI, then ask directly — is there enough cash for this hire, what is the current burn rate, how many months of inventory remain — and insist on graphs and dashboards.

**Saved tutorials that never land.** He calls collecting tutorials "organised procrastination". The fix: paste the link into the AI and ask whether it is useful for you — it already knows your goals and systems — then implement it immediately if it is; better still, turn that judgement into an automated skill so future links are triaged automatically.

## 9. Assistants and debugging

**Building an AI assistant.** Three layers: connect the APIs of all your systems; run an agent layer that keeps them working together — watching the calendar, the inbox, Slack; then give the assistant an identity, with behaviour rules and security boundaries. He separates two forms: single-player mode for yourself, and multiplayer mode for a team, where company data is integrated, correlated and cleaned into a single source of truth that dashboards and task execution sit on.

**Code errors.** The core rule is that the AI has to see the problem to solve it. If the error is not visible, have the program generate an error window you can screenshot during development; if the fix still fails, give the screenshot to a different model and let the two check each other.

## Comprehension check

1. **Why is AI's default answer usually generic?**
   Answer: It is trained on the full human corpus and condenses the best answer into an average, without the specifics of your situation — so the prompt has to supply mission, parameters and output shape.

2. **What does each letter of MAPS require?**
   Answer: Mission is the goal behind the task; Ask is the concrete deliverable; Parameters is material about your situation, up to several books' worth; Shape is the form of the output.

3. **How do you stop re-introducing yourself in every new chat?**
   Answer: Connect email, cloud drive and notes, have the AI generate a detailed master prompt about you, correct it, save it into settings, and paste it into your other AI tools.

4. **What do red, yellow and green mean on the daily dashboard?**
   Answer: Red means below 10% of the required activity; yellow means within 10% of it; green means on track.

5. **How does the DRY rule apply to AI workflows?**
   Answer: The second time a task comes up, stop doing it manually — record and narrate it, have the AI convert the recording into a skill, then schedule that skill.

6. **Why is financial data hard to use with AI?**
   Answer: Most accounting tools lack usable AI interfaces and operators often will not organise the data, so input quality is poor. The fix is to connect bank and payment systems first, then ask questions and visualise.

7. **What is the first step when code throws an error?**
   Answer: Make the error visible — add a development-time error window you can screenshot, or capture the error message and hand it to the AI; if that fails, give the screenshot to a second model.
