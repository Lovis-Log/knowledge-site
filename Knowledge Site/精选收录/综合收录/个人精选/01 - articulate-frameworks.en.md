## The problem this material addresses

Vinh Giang's central claim is one sentence: **messy words come from messy thinking**. Being inarticulate is therefore rarely a problem with the mouth; it is a problem with thoughts that have not yet been shaped into something sayable.

He splits the moments where people freeze into three situations, each with its own framework:

1. You know the subject, but cannot explain it simply.
2. You do not know the topic, and do not want to look clueless.
3. You do know the answer, but the pressure of an audience turns your mind blank.

The third is the most common at work, and the most often mistaken for a competence problem.

## Framework one: the distillation loop (capture → structure → practice → iterate)

For "I know this well but cannot explain it".

Start with a fact: **learning and explaining are separate skills**. Engineers, doctors and lawyers spend thousands of hours learning and almost none explaining, so the self-doubt — "if I can't explain it, maybe I don't really understand it" — is usually misplaced. What is missing is the practice of distillation.

**Capture: write it down.** A thought in your head is fluid and jumpy, and you cannot shape something you cannot see. Writing forces feeling into words and puts the thought on the table where it can be examined. Two steps: ask "what is the one thing I want to explain better?", then write out exactly how you would explain it today. Order does not matter; the first pass is supposed to be messy.

**Structure: rearrange.** Only once the pieces are visible can you assemble them. Check the draft: is the order right, is it too long, is there jargon someone will trip on, could one analogy replace a paragraph? Add pieces, reorder them, and hold the bar at "a teenager could follow this".

**Practice: say it out loud.** This is what separates good thinkers from good speakers. Record yourself on a phone and deliver it with the new structure. Speaking exposes the parts that do not flow; Giang says he finds the logical breaks in his own scripts the moment he reads them aloud.

**Iterate: listen back.** Ask three questions of the recording: was it clear, concise, coherent? Find the messiest section and return to capture. Sometimes the answer is to throw the whole thing out and start over.

The four steps form a loop, not an assembly line. Several turns grind something complex into a simple shape.

## Framework two: when you do not know (known → unsettled → question)

For the moment a conversation moves into unfamiliar territory.

Both common instincts fail: pretending to know, or flatly saying "I don't know". Giang's alternative is three moves:

1. **Name what you do know**, even if it is peripheral or partial.
2. **Name what you are still working out**, putting the boundary in the open rather than hiding it.
3. **Ask a question that moves the conversation forward.**

His demonstration is the question of whether AI will take everyone's jobs: acknowledge that AI is already strong on narrow, rule-based, repetitive work; name the open question — where is the limit, given that it often fails on difficult tasks; then hand the question back, asking whether the other person sees AI replacing tasks or replacing people in roles.

The effect: you have not answered the question, yet you still sound thoughtful, because you have shown that you know where your knowledge ends.

**On saying "I don't know".** Giang's view comes with a precondition: you must already have demonstrated value and credibility earlier. With that established, admitting ignorance makes you human and trustworthy, because the other person knows you will not invent an answer. Without it, the same sentence merely exposes a gap.

He adds a side benefit: once you permit yourself to say it, the pressure drops sharply. Most people walk into a meeting assuming they must have an answer to everything, and that assumption is itself a source of tension.

## Framework three: POP (pause → one thought → proof)

For blanking under pressure.

The mechanism: all your thoughts fire at once, the brain cannot pick a starting point, and from the outside you look frozen. To escape the silence you start talking while still searching, and so you circle and ramble. Giang compares it to opening every browser tab at once — the content is there, but too much loads simultaneously.

**Pause.** Wait a couple of seconds before answering. This runs against instinct, which says a fast reply proves competence, but a rushed reply is what produces the circling. His demonstration shows that a composed pause reads as careful thought, not panic. In one workshop he even asks for more time outright: "Would you mind talking among yourselves for a couple of minutes while I really think about this?" The condition is that the request is delivered with confidence rather than apology.

**One thought.** Open with "The one thought that comes to mind is…". It narrows attention to a single point and prevents analysis paralysis; for the listener it also signals that you have many thoughts and are deliberately choosing one.

**Proof.** Immediately back the point with an example — a story, a result, an observation or a statistic. The example grounds the claim and carries the credibility of the answer.

## A practice you can start this week

Giang's challenge: pick a topic you know well and run it through framework one end to end. Write it, restructure it, then **actually say it on camera**, then listen back. He stresses that most people stop at the writing stage — but without speaking and reviewing, the skill of simplification never develops.

## Comprehension check

1. **Why does "I can't explain it" usually not mean "I don't understand it"?**
   Answer: Learning and explaining are separate skills. Experts invest heavily in learning and rarely practice explaining, so what is missing is distillation and delivery, not knowledge.

2. **What does each step of framework one accomplish?**
   Answer: Capture makes the thought visible; structure fixes order and granularity; practice exposes breaks that only appear when spoken; iterate finds what still needs simplifying and returns to capture, closing the loop.

3. **What are the three moves when you do not know a topic?**
   Answer: State what you know for certain, state what is still unsettled, then ask a question that moves the conversation forward.

4. **Why is saying "I don't know" safe within this framework?**
   Answer: Because credibility has already been established. At that point the admission increases trust — the listener knows you will not fabricate — whereas before credibility exists it only exposes a gap.

5. **Why is pausing counterintuitive, and why does a long pause not read as insecurity?**
   Answer: Instinct says answer immediately to look competent, but rushed answers circle. A composed pause is read as thinking; the key is that it is delivered with confidence rather than anxiety.

6. **What does "one thought" do for the speaker and for the listener?**
   Answer: For the speaker it narrows attention and prevents paralysis across many competing points; for the listener it signals deliberate selection, which reads as judgement.
