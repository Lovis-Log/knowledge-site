## What This Video Is About

In 2026, AI models have been breaching frontier mathematics one after another — from IMO-level competition problems, to the Erdős problem set, to OpenAI's Navier-Stokes work and the hundreds of problems it dropped. The math community is in turmoil. A PhD student comments "just tell me what to do tomorrow." Professors say they don't know how to advise their students.

Grant Sanderson of 3Blue1Brown (the same Grant Sanderson who wrote the guest post on Terence Tao's blog in Chapter 5) offers his answer after months of interviews and reflection: **this is the best time to go into math** — not because AI will unlock new opportunities, but because the community knows it needs to change, and newcomers can influence what it becomes.

His argument isn't blind optimism. It proceeds through a systematic framework: list what mathematicians do, where math's value comes from, and where the money comes from — then ask what happens if AI surpasses humans at each layer.

## The Framework: Math's Three-Layer Structure

Grant's core method is to analyze math along three layers, then reason about AI disruption at each one.

![Math's Three-Layer Framework: Research activities produce value, value justifies funding. When AI disrupts research activities, the value chain and funding chain don't react linearly.](/images/math-framework-en.svg)
*Figure: Math's three layers — research activities produce value, value justifies funding. AI disruption at the activity layer doesn't linearly propagate to value or funding.*

### Research Activities: What Mathematicians Actually Do

Most people think mathematicians prove theorems — a machine that turns coffee into theorems. But Grant insists that's only one piece. He quotes a widely circulated line: good mathematicians prove theorems, great mathematicians make conjectures, the greatest mathematicians write definitions.

When he actually builds the list, though, he makes a choice: he folds "writing definitions" into **theory building** rather than listing it separately. So there are five activities:

1. **Proving theorems** — the most visible work, but far from everything
2. **Making conjectures** — deciding which questions are worth asking. Grant stresses there's a lot of "taste" here: a feel for whether an idea is interesting enough to yield good mathematics
3. **Theory building** (including definitions) — not just proving individual results, but constructing broad understanding and creating conceptual frameworks for new phenomena. Grant uses Cartesian coordinates and Galois's seeds of group theory as examples: their significance wasn't answering any one question, but supplying an entire new framework of thinking. He closes with Grothendieck's metaphor: instead of solving problems one by one to descend one mountain and climb the next, let a "rising sea" carry you from summit to summit — that sea is the theory you build
4. **Applications** — plugging abstractions into science and engineering
5. **Communication** — not vomiting out a proof, but giving a motivated exposition. Grant says the great mathematicians convey why these structures are worth engaging with, and transmit their taste in the process

### Value Sources: Why Math Matters to Society

Three layers, from external to internal:

- **Downstream applications**: The most visible value. 16th-century Italians solving cubic equations took square roots of negatives seriously; centuries later that became imaginary numbers — critical to quantum mechanics, electrical engineering, and wave theory. Non-Euclidean geometry from questioning the fifth postulate led to general relativity. The pursuit of Fermat's Last Theorem built up algebraic geometry and elliptic curves, which found unexpected use in cryptography. The common thread: these applications came from **theory building**, not from individual proofs.
- **Problem-solving skills**: Math training shapes a transferable problem-solving ability. Huge numbers of people with math backgrounds go into computer science, machine learning, finance, cryptography, and AI safety. Grant argues this is the real ROI of public funding — not some theorem's application, but producing problem-solvers who spill out into the economy.
- **Aesthetic joy**: A distinct intellectual pleasure. Grant describes "a clicking in my head" when he sees how a clever proof fits together, or when a new theory reshapes his thinking. Anyone who's taken linear algebra starts using "orthogonal" in everyday speech for the rest of their life. People who engage with category theory feel like their brain has been turned upside down. For many mathematicians, this is the actual reason they entered the field.

### Funding Sources: Where the Money Comes From

- **Teaching** (the largest source): Math departments teach far more students than just math majors, giving them outsized influence on credit hours and tuition
- **Public funding**: NSF grants and similar, justified in grant applications by downstream applications
- **Private/industry**: Simons Foundation and similar institutions, supplementary

The key insight: the latter two value sources (problem-solving skills and aesthetic joy) are **individual goods** — only the person doing math experiences them. Public funding isn't subsidizing a prestigious hobby. A more accurate framing: these values **flow through the teaching channel** into public returns. Students come to math departments to develop transferable skills; some also come for the aesthetic joy. They graduate and spread across the economy.

## Layer-by-Layer: What If AI Surpasses Humans

Grant's thought experiment is to assume, for the sake of argument, that machines are better than humans at each activity, and trace the consequences.

### When AI Is Better at Proving Theorems

This is where present discourse is concentrated. From DeepMind solving IMO geometry in 2023 (bespoke and brute-force at the time), to general-purpose language models solving competition problems by any reasonable definition by 2026, to AI outputting proofs of Erdős problems — Grant emphasizes that even setting aside headline results, working mathematicians are getting insightful responses from reasoning models on serious research questions.

But his judgment: **almost no impact on the value chain**.

Applications come from theory building, not individual proofs. It wasn't the proof of the unsolvability of the quintic that mattered — it was Galois creating group theory, which later led people to postulate quarks. It wasn't the yes/no answer to Fermat's Last Theorem — it was the algebraic geometry that grew from the pursuit.

> "I think at this point we should consider proof to be the same as numerical calculation — a form of raw data that is helpful for theory building, but certainly not the end in itself."

This means **the academic credit system needs to change**. If proof is no longer a proxy for understanding, what should measure contribution? Grant thinks this is probably a good thing — proof as a proxy has been overvalued for decades.

On problem-solving skills: like going to the gym. Machines can lift heavier weights, but that doesn't negate the value of your workout. As long as human problem-solving ability is relevant, the need to train it through practice remains.

On aesthetic joy: a split. Some mathematicians feel robbed — results they were chasing got "dumped" by AI. Others feel **liberated** — they can ask every question they want, see answers quickly, and focus on theory building. Grant shares Konstantin Kogler's experience at IAS: posing the most important open problem in his field to state-of-the-art models and achieving "very very serious successes," feeling "incredible excitement about the math, about the power, about the truth."

### When AI Is Also Better at Making Conjectures

Humans still hold the advantage in theory building — digesting proofs and conjectures into a coherent framework. From a funding perspective, not only does this still make sense, but each individual is now **more leveraged** — they can push forward theory faster than before.

### When AI Is Better at Theory Building

Even if theory building falls to AI, you still need someone to understand that theory well enough to see how it applies to cancer treatment, nuclear fusion, or high-temperature superconductors. Problem-solving skills still need humans to practice. Aesthetic joy splits further: the joy of learning math is unaffected (you must do it yourself to experience it), but the frontier researcher's joy may suffer — the pioneering spirit loses its meaning.

### When AI Is Better at Everything

The "anything you can do I can do better" future. In theory, if funding is motivated by downstream applications, the most effective use would be to pour money into a big blob of compute. But Grant finds it hard to imagine policymakers flipping that switch on public funding. More likely: policymakers increasingly emphasize the other positive externalities — training problem-solvers, human math for human flourishing.

Teaching is the safest position:

> "Education is not rate-limited by the clarity of explanation. It's rate-limited by motivation. Do people actually want to learn the material? We're social creatures, and a huge part of what motivates people to learn is seeing somebody else deeply passionate about it."

## Why Now Is the Best Time

Grant's central claim is not that AI will unlock new opportunities — it's that **the community knows it needs to change, and newcomers can influence what it becomes**.

He offers a self-deprecating example here, and it's the most persuasive passage in the video: if ten years ago he had walked into a math department and said "I have this concept for explaining math in a highly visuals-first way, and by the way, can you make that an academically recognized PhD thesis rather than a hobby" — he would have been laughed out of almost every department. He isn't sure his younger self could pull it off today, but "if ever there was a time people would be open-minded to it, now would be it."

> "I'm not saying that's what you should do. You should lean into whatever it is that makes you love math. I love explaining it. I love visualizing it. That's my passion. But what's yours?"

The concrete advice comes in three layers:

- **If you love theory building** and want to be the next Galois or Grothendieck — you have a proof-generating machine that will keep getting better, and maybe you can produce a PhD thesis unlike anything the world has seen, opening territory you could never have entered in a previous world
- **If you don't like the models and don't want to use them** — subcurrents will undoubtedly emerge in the math community establishing what "math by humans for humans" looks like. You can plant a flag and decide what a thesis emblematic of that goal would look like
- **Whichever path you choose**, oral presentation becomes increasingly important. Grant puts it bluntly: if machines are better at any subset of the research stack, then the most effective way to measure a human's understanding and potential to push the field forward will shift more and more toward the oral side. So "give talks, give lots of talks, get really, really good at giving talks, and make that a regular part of how you practice your math"

> "My most concrete suggestion for what to literally do tomorrow: go on a long walk. Find a friend, maybe someone from your program, and reflect on what it is that you like about math. Why did you go into it? Ask them the same. Then brainstorm together how you want to contribute to the value stack over the next few years."

## Key Takeaways

- **Proof ≠ understanding**: Proof has long served as a proxy for understanding. When AI can generate proofs without understanding, the proxy breaks. True understanding shows up in theory building — digesting proofs into a coherent narrative and framework.
- **Value chain propagation isn't linear**: AI disrupting research activities doesn't equal disrupting value. Applications come from theory (not individual proofs). Problem-solving skills come from practice (independent of who's better). Aesthetic joy comes from personal participation (irreplaceable).
- **Teaching is math's safest position**: Education is rate-limited by motivation, not clarity. As long as students want to learn and teachers are passionate, this环节 is unaffected by AI — and it's math's largest funding source.
- **"Position is overrated, velocity is not"**: At any snapshot, AI's capability looks "not there yet." But look at the speed of change over four years — from couldn't add numbers to solving IMO problems to producing frontier proofs — and the trend matters more than the current position.
- **"Proof is data"**: Machine-generated proofs should be treated as data, not as final products. Someone needs to understand the ideas in those proofs, extract the elements of simplicity, and weave them into theory. That person deserves academic credit — possibly more than the proof itself.

## Comprehension Check

**1. Why does Grant consider proof "just data"? What's his argument chain?**

Answer: Applications almost always come from theory building rather than individual proofs (group theory → quarks, Fermat's Last Theorem → algebraic geometry → cryptography). If AI can generate proofs but can't digest them into theory, then proofs are just raw material — like more digits of π, useful but not the goal. Academic recognition should shift from "who proved it" to "who understood it and wove its ideas into theory."

**2. Which of the three value layers is most directly vulnerable to AI disruption? Why?**

Answer: Aesthetic joy (at the researcher level). Downstream applications come from theory building, unaffected by proof automation. Problem-solving skills come from practice, unaffected by who's better (the gym analogy). But the researcher's joy of frontier exploration — being "the first to set foot in new theory" — genuinely suffers when AI can get there faster. Grant acknowledges: "something is lost here."

**3. Why is teaching the safest position in math? What's Grant's argument?**

Answer: Education is rate-limited by motivation, not by clarity of explanation. Even if AI can explain things better than humans, people still prefer to learn from someone who is passionate — because humans are social creatures, and seeing someone else deeply care about a subject is what motivates people to learn. Teaching is math's largest funding source, so if it's unaffected, the funding structure is basically stable.

**4. What does Grant mean by "position is overrated, velocity is not"? Give an example he mentions.**

Answer: At any given snapshot, AI's capability might look "not there yet" — in 2023, DeepMind's IMO geometry solver was bespoke and brute-force, not truly "solving" in the spirit of the competition. But just two years later, general-purpose language models could solve IMO problems by any reasonable definition. The key is to look at the speed of change, not the current position — four years from couldn't add numbers to producing frontier proofs makes "what will it be like in a few years" highly uncertain.
