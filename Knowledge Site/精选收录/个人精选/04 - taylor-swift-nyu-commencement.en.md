## Leaving a framework without receiving a script

Speaking on behalf of the honorary degree recipients at NYU's 2022 commencement, Taylor Swift offers ways of approaching choices rather than a prescribed life plan. Her themes include deciding what deserves space in one's life, allowing oneself to look awkward, investing openly in one's interests, and repairing mistakes. She ends by acknowledging that nobody can know the right answer in advance at every turning point.

This is an independent learning note covering the major arguments and examples, not a full transcript. The clipping dates the ceremony to May 18, 2022, while May 26 is the video's publication date.

## Gratitude without pretending life followed the plan

The patchwork-quilt metaphor places a person inside a history of relationships. People who offered love, belief, kindness, or difficult truths have contributed to who that person becomes. Swift adds that these supporters may not have acted perfectly and may no longer be present. Gratitude does not require an idealized account of every relationship.

She then describes missing a conventional college experience: doing schoolwork in airports and traveling with her mother for radio promotion. She recognizes that graduates who lived through dorm restrictions and remote learning also missed the experience they had imagined. That acknowledgment connects her unusual biography to a specific audience rather than assuming a celebrity's problems are universal.

Her delivery-menu analogy describes the gap between the life one orders and the life one receives. She praises what the graduates have done with their circumstances rather than claiming those circumstances were ideal. **Additional interpretation:** acknowledging losses and recognizing one's capacity to respond can coexist. Recognizing capacity need not erase the loss.

## Discernment: deciding what deserves room

Swift presents life as a load that becomes excessive if everything must be carried at once. Grudges, updates about an ex, and comparisons with a former bully's career all compete for space. One harmful relationship can outweigh many ordinary pleasures.

The argument concerns the allocation of attention and room in one's life. It does not imply that every difficult commitment should be abandoned: the same speech endorses effort, responsibility, and facing mistakes. Difficulty alone cannot determine whether something deserves to be kept.

**Additional exercise:** choose one recurring concern and separate the facts, actions still available, and repetitive thoughts that add no new information. Keeping responsibility for an action need not mean feeding every cycle of resentment or comparison. This is a proposed learning exercise, not a psychological model supplied by Swift.

## Allowing embarrassment makes room for effort

Swift also discusses the inevitability of cringe. Swift recalls a period of dressing in a particular retro style: it may seem funny later, but it was enjoyable then. Changing tastes and identities make retrospective embarrassment difficult to avoid. Treating the prevention of all future embarrassment as a decision rule can inhibit present experimentation.

She moves from private discomfort to social judgment. A culture of apparent indifference can make visible desire seem uncool. She challenges that judgment and contrasts the detached people she wanted to know in high school with the highly motivated people she now wants to hire.

Two kinds of discomfort must remain distinct. Awkwardness about an outfit or an earnest attempt may be something to live with. Shame after harming another person calls for responsibility. Her later discussion of apology and repair prevents acceptance of imperfection from becoming permission to ignore consequences.

Similarly, enthusiasm is not a guarantee of success. The speech encourages effort and questions the status attached to looking effortless; it does not establish that wanting an outcome intensely enough will produce it.

## A creative compass can support changing forms

Swift describes songwriting, which she began at twelve, as a continuing compass. Directing, developing tour visuals, and performing are connected by the craft of writing. She names the labor within that craft: selecting ideas, polishing them, editing, and discarding an older idea when a better one appears.

Her movement across country, pop, alternative, and folk illustrates a willingness to change creative positions. She extends the observation beyond music. Social posts, a thesis, and emails to a boss or a close friend use different voices.

**Additional analysis:** changing one's register does not necessarily mean abandoning one's values. A useful check is whether the underlying facts and judgments remain consistent while tone and detail change for an audience. Adapting one's voice should not become a reason to invent a different truth for each reader.

She presents the ability to choose as both welcome and frightening. Autonomy allows experimentation, but it also removes the expectation that an external script will settle every question.

## How rejection becomes a new direction

Swift explains her reluctance to prescribe choices for others. Entering public life at fifteen brought years of unsolicited warnings and pressure to be a flawless young female role model. She challenges the assumption that a mistake destroys a person's chance at a rewarding life.

Her examples supply intermediate actions rather than merely declaring adversity useful:

| Experience | Response or learning in her account |
| --- | --- |
| Exclusion from hometown gatherings | Spending time writing songs that helped create a route elsewhere |
| Labels dismissing a thirteen-year-old country singer | Posting on MySpace and communicating with young country listeners |
| Harsh external accounts of her identity | Looking inward to understand who she was |
| Public scrutiny of her relationships | Protecting her private life more firmly |
| Repeated public humiliation | Reducing the importance attached to fluctuating visibility and approval |

The recurring mechanism involves a changed channel, creative response, or boundary after rejection. **Additional analysis:** suffering alone does not produce opportunity. Subsequent action, support, and circumstances matter. These are retrospective accounts from a successful person, not evidence that everyone facing similar harm will receive the same benefit. They cannot justify asking people to be grateful for mistreatment.

Later in the address, she also rejects the image of constant optimism. She admits losing perspective and sometimes feeling that things are pointless. Omitting this admission would distort the speech into a demand to feel positive at all times.

## Imperfection still carries responsibility

Swift lists errors that include misplaced trust, overreaction, hurting others, self-sabotage, denying wrongdoing, and becoming consumed by guilt. The sequence turns toward addressing the pain caused and trying to do better next time.

Two propositions therefore stand together: a mistake need not end a life, and a mistake can cause real losses and injuries. Her suggestion that losses can accompany gains does not cancel the losses.

**Additional review practice:** describe what happened, identify who was affected, determine available repair, and change the next action. Saying that everyone is imperfect skips responsibility if it ends the discussion. Endless self-condemnation without action also leaves the repair undone. This sequence is an application developed from the speech, not a procedure Swift explicitly names.

## Choosing when no maxim can decide for you

The paired situations Swift describes define the limits of universal advice. Sometimes one should stand up for oneself; sometimes apologize. Sometimes fight; sometimes leave. Some situations require abandoning older ideas for reform, while others call for listening to earlier generations.

Extracting persistence or letting go as the single lesson would remove these qualifications. Swift declines to tell every graduate which option will be right at each critical moment.

**Additional exercise:** before a difficult choice, write down the present goal, affected people, known information, missing information, and one next step that can still be revised. The aim is not certainty. It is a decision with reasons that can later be examined and improved.

The closing references to recovery and breathing bring the scale back to the present. Difficulty will happen; people need to recover and continue. Breathing functions here as a closing image and a joke about the honorary doctorate, not a clinical recommendation.

## Check your understanding

1. **How does discernment differ from avoiding effort?**

   Reference answer: discernment asks which relationships, comparisons, and burdens deserve room. Effort concerns sustained action toward something worth doing. The speech endorses both; ease is not its sole criterion of value.

2. **Why does accepting cringe not mean ignoring mistakes?**

   Reference answer: awkwardness about a style or an earnest attempt can be tolerated as part of change. Harm to another person requires acknowledgment and repair. The later passages explicitly address pain caused and doing better.

3. **What connects label rejection to a later opportunity in Swift's example?**

   Reference answer: she changed channels by posting songs on MySpace and connecting with peers who liked country music. The mechanism includes continued creative action; rejection itself does not guarantee success.

4. **Must different writing voices imply an unstable identity?**

   Reference answer: no. A thesis, a social post, and emails to different recipients need different forms. Tone can change while facts and underlying judgments remain consistent. Swift uses writing to illustrate the plurality of everyday expression.

5. **Why can the speech endorse both standing firm and backing down?**

   Reference answer: the right response depends on the situation, responsibilities, and aims. No single strategy resolves every choice. Autonomy brings freedom alongside the risk of acting without advance certainty.

6. **What limits should accompany learning from setbacks?**

   Reference answer: setbacks do not guarantee a reward, a successful person's retrospective account does not explain everyone's experience, and learning does not eliminate loss or responsibility. Look for actionable responses without romanticizing harm.
