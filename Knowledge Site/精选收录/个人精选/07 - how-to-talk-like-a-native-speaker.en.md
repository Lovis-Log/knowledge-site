## What this talk is asking

Marc Green got lost in Moscow at fifteen because he could not read Russian; over the following years he learned four more languages. The talk is not about how to start a foreign language. It asks a narrower question: can a learner move past fluency and obtain what normally belongs only to native speakers — being treated as one of them, and even taking on a second personality?

His answer is yes, and the academic notion of mastery is not required. The path compresses into three kinds of work: fix the accent, learn what locals actually say, and internalize cultural reflexes.

## From the Moscow metro to four more languages

In 1987, at fifteen, he was part of a group of Westerners touring the Soviet Union. One evening he and a friend slipped past security and went down into the metro. The Moscow system is the deepest in the world; the escalator ride alone takes a full minute. He did what felt prudent: wrote the station name in a notepad so they could retrace their route.

Half an hour later they wanted to go back. He showed the note to one person, then another, then a third, and got three different directions. Then he saw the sign above the stairs. The word he had copied meant “exit.”

Above ground they found a taxi, said “Intourist Hotel,” and handed the driver 50 rubles. The driver looked at him and said: No, dollar. Fifty dollars was roughly twenty times what he had offered. They could not pay, and the driver left them on a cold street. They walked to the end of the block, turned the corner, and the hotel stood two hundred yards ahead.

Two things stayed with him: a reflexive flinch every time he heard Russian afterwards, and a concrete understanding that knowing the local language is not a decorative extra when you travel. Over the following years he learned four more languages fluently.

## Three markers on one deck of cards

His model for language ability is a deck of cards lying face down. Learning turns them over, and the proportion turned up marks the stages.

- **About 25%: basic.** A base vocabulary, some grammar, and the ability to hold very simple conversations and communicate a little.
- **More than 50%: fluent.** The language becomes part of the subconscious. Even after ten years or more without use it is not forgotten, and it can be recovered in a very short time; thinking in the language feels comfortable.
- **Higher still: mastery.** Familiarity with the literature and in-depth knowledge of specialized fields — usually the standard academia cares about.

The decisive point is the third stage: **the practical benefits of a native speaker do not require academic mastery.** Many native speakers have neither specialized knowledge nor an elaborate vocabulary. Mastery is therefore not a gate, and the whole step can be skipped.

## What bilingualism gives first: a second identity

He was raised by a German-speaking mother and an American father, and only around age two worked out the rule that women spoke only German and men only English. Being bilingual gave him a head start on his first foreign language, but something else mattered more: two identities and the ability to switch between them.

Personality, humor, and a value system shift with the language in use. He cites studies suggesting better problem-solving and greater resistance to Alzheimer's disease among bilinguals, but he cares more about the social payoff: among native speakers you feel at home, and they accept you as one of their own.

That turns the talk into a question about learners: can someone studying a foreign language also develop another identity and enjoy the social benefits that go beyond communication skills?

## Three kinds of work between fluency and native-like

### 1. Fix the accent

He calls this the most overlooked and most important aspect of language learning today. Speaking with no accent, or almost none, changes how native speakers respond to you unconsciously, and it lets you adapt to a new self-image.

His method is the “perfect sentence” drill:

1. Find a native speaker to help.
2. Open a book in the target language at a random page and read the first sentence.
3. Ask the native speaker to rate you on three levels: obvious accent, slight accent, no accent.
4. Have the native speaker read the sentence back; listen closely and repeat.
5. Repeat until the native speaker can no longer hear an accent.

He admits a single sentence can take a very long time. But with persistence, he says, the change in your accent will surprise you.

### 2. Learn the words locals actually use

Textbook vocabulary and street vocabulary are often two different sets. His French examples, with the transcript's misspellings restored:

| Textbook | Everyday | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| le travail | mon boulot | work |
| les vêtements | les fringues | clothes |
| l'argent | le fric / le sou | money |

There is no system for these; they have to be learned one by one, and only through interaction with native speakers. Once a critical mass is reached, new expressions get picked up in one go, the way native speakers absorb unfamiliar words.

### 3. Make cultural reflexes your own

The same gesture can be rude in one culture, incredulous in another, a request for food somewhere else, and simply “please wait” in the Middle East. Reactions to pain differ too: Americans say “Ouch,” Germans “Ow-ah,” the French “Ay.” For agreement, an American says “Uh-huh,” a German “Mm-hmm,” a Frenchman “Hmm.”

None of this appears in textbooks. It requires active listening and observation until the reflexes become yours. All three areas, he stresses, depend on interacting with native speakers as much as possible.

## When there are no native speakers around

Full immersion is the ideal: live abroad for a while, spend time among native speakers at home, have a romantic relationship, or simply work alongside them.

Even without that, learning should not stop:

- Watch films and TV shows, mimic the characters, write down anything unfamiliar and practice it.
- Learn song lyrics. Songs tell stories, singing trains pronunciation, and emotionally charged lyrics anchor expressions into active vocabulary.

The last ingredient is a mindset: the belief that sounding, expressing, talking, and acting like a native actually produces a native-like level.

## If you take away one thing

Work on your pronunciation. It helps at every stage, speeds up everything else, and is the key to reaching a native or near-native level.

He closes with a joke about how he overcame his fear of Russian: he married a Russian woman, and now small children speak Russian to him every day at home.

## Check your understanding

1. **What stages does the deck-of-cards model define, and what marks each one?**

   Reference answer: About 25% turned up is basic — core vocabulary and simple conversation. More than 50% is fluency, where the language enters the subconscious, survives long disuse, and returns quickly. Above that is mastery, tied to literature and specialized knowledge. He stresses that the third stage is not required for native-level benefits.

2. **What are the steps of the “perfect sentence” drill, and why does it work?**

   Reference answer: Read a random sentence to a native speaker, have them rate it as obvious, slight, or no accent, listen to their model, and repeat until no accent remains. It isolates very small units of sound and uses immediate feedback to overwrite pronunciation habits.

3. **Why is memorizing vocabulary lists not enough to sound local?**

   Reference answer: Textbook words and spoken words are often separate sets, as with le travail and mon boulot. The replacements follow no general rule, must be acquired one at a time, and depend on real interaction; only after a critical mass do new expressions get absorbed in one go.

4. **What exactly is the “second identity,” and what does it provide?**

   Reference answer: It is the shift in personality, humor, and values that follows the language in use. It brings ease among native speakers, acceptance as one of them, and the cognitive and social advantages he cites. He uses it to pose the central question: whether a learner can acquire that identity too.

5. **Without native speakers nearby, how does he suggest continuing?**

   Reference answer: Mimic characters in films and TV, record and practice unfamiliar expressions, learn emotionally resonant song lyrics, and keep the belief that a native-like level is reachable. These substitute for interaction but do not fully replace it.
