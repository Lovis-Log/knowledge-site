> **The short version:** the most useful fact for a beginner is that you do not have to pick the right company. Buying the whole market consistently and leaving it alone for long enough has historically beaten most active approaches. The hard part is not stock selection; it is staying invested and keeping costs low.

## Why this guide exists

Humphrey Yang is a former financial advisor who later worked in gaming, tech and e-commerce. This is a video of just over fifty minutes, structured like a short course: first why to invest, then how much the arithmetic can produce, then the vocabulary, how stocks are grouped, how to build a portfolio, which account to hold it in, how to research a company, and finally two live brokerage walkthroughs and the tax rules that follow a sale.

Everything about accounts, taxes, tickers and order rules here is **specific to the United States** — the S&P 500, Roth IRA, 401(k), Form 1099, and two US brokerages. The compounding arithmetic, the case for index funds, diversification and the argument against market timing travel well; the account and tax mechanics do not. The video was published on 1 February 2025 and the live demos were recorded on Friday, 17 January 2025, so dollar figures and contribution limits are those of early 2025, not today's.

The question the video answers is narrow and practical: with a few hundred dollars and no financial training, how do you start without turning the market into a casino?

## The case for investing is a case against cash

Yang's first argument is not that stocks go up; it is that sitting in cash quietly loses. Three costs stack up.

**Opportunity cost.** Yang uses 8–10% as a rough historical annual-return figure for the S&P 500, without specifying the exact calculation window or return series. His comparison uses hypothetical rates: on one million dollars, 0.1% is 1,000 dollars in a year, 5% is 50,000, and 10% is 100,000. These figures illustrate the arithmetic; savings rates and actual annual equity returns can change.

**Inflation.** The Federal Reserve publicly targets 2% inflation a year, yet in the years right after the pandemic the news was reporting closer to 5–8%. If your money earns less than inflation, the balance is unchanged and the purchasing power is shrinking. His illustration: a first-class stamp cost 8 cents in 1971 and 73 cents at the time of recording — the same service at roughly nine times the price.

**The asset comparison.** A street-interview clip ranks stocks first at about 5.2% and real estate at about 3% over a century. The clip does not give the underlying series or say whether returns are adjusted for inflation. Its ranking and Yang’s S&P 500 illustration use different sources.

## Compounding: the calculation that carries the video

Start with the small example, because it isolates the mechanism. Put in \$1,000 at a 10% annual return: year one ends at \$1,100, year two at \$1,210 — the second year's 10% is earned on \$1,100, not on the original \$1,000. Repeat for 20 years and \$1,000 becomes about \$6,727. That is "interest earning interest", but the scale is too small to change behaviour.

The main example is the one to actually check. The assumptions are explicit:

- **Starting deposit:** \$5,000 already sitting in savings
- **Recurring contribution:** \$1,000 every month, added at the end of each month
- **Assumed return:** 8% a year, compounded monthly (8% ÷ 12 per month)
- **Term:** 30 years, i.e. 360 monthly contributions

Under those assumptions the initial \$5,000 grows to about \$54,679, using the same monthly rate as the deposits: \$5,000 × (1 + 0.08/12)³⁶⁰. The month-end deposits grow to about \$1.49 million. Together they come to about \$1.545 million on \$365,000 of contributions, for about \$1.18 million in hypothetical growth.

Three consequences are worth keeping:

- **Growth is back-loaded.** On a 30-year curve the first decade looks almost flat and the last decade steepens sharply. Quitting halfway costs you exactly the steepest segment.
- **Doubling the contribution roughly doubles the endpoint**, because the extra money also compounds: at \$2,000 a month under the same assumptions the balance approaches \$3 million, not \$1.5 million.
- **Time is the only input you fully control.** You cannot choose the market's return and your contribution is bounded by income; starting early and not interrupting are the two decisions available to everyone.

One necessary qualification: 8% is an average, not an annual promise. Yang names the spread himself — the S&P 500 fell close to 18% in 2022, rose around 20% in 2023 and about 25% in 2024. An index fund removes stock-picking risk, not market risk.

### Why "keep buying" beats waiting for a cheaper market

Yang draws on Nick Maggiulli’s *Just Keep Buying* when discussing the relationship between valuation and subsequent returns. His account is about historical US stock data with dividends reinvested and returns adjusted for inflation. He compares rolling 20- and 30-year holding periods to illustrate how the relationship with entry valuation changes with horizon. The note does not establish the exact sample window.

Yang illustrates the cost of waiting with the S&P 500 index level: around 1,400 in 2012 and near 6,000 when he recorded the video in early 2025. That comparison is a price-level illustration over roughly 13 years; it does not include dividends or inflation adjustment.

## A working vocabulary

Seven terms cover most financial news. The memory hooks are Yang's.

| Term | Meaning | Hook |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bull / bear | Expecting prices to rise / to fall | Bulls thrust their horns up; bears swipe their claws down |
| Market cap | Share price × shares outstanding; the size of the company | Sets its volatility and how much room it has to grow |
| Index | Tracks a preselected basket of investments | S&P 500, Nasdaq, Dow Jones |
| Frothy | Valuations inflating like bubbles rising in a glass | Not yet a bubble, but getting close |
| Blue chip | Large, stable, established company (Apple, Coca-Cola, McDonald's, Microsoft) | From the most valuable poker chips; first used in 1923 for shares above \$200 |
| Penny stock | Tiny, highly speculative company trading for pennies | Where beginners most easily lose their capital |
| Dividend | A share of profit returned to shareholders | Usually quarterly, in cash or reinvested stock |

A **bubble** is froth taken further: prices inflated by speculation until the bubble bursts. Yang's example is the dot-com era around 2000–2001, when companies with no underlying business traded at extreme valuations.

## Three ways stocks get grouped

**By market cap.** Under \$300 million is a micro cap; \$300 million–\$2 billion a small cap; \$2–10 billion a mid cap; over \$10 billion a large cap, with mega caps above that (Apple, Nvidia, Alphabet). Market cap predicts the risk profile: micro caps can multiply or go to zero, while a large cap is steadier but has limited upside — for Apple, then valued above \$3 trillion, doubling would mean creating another \$3 trillion of value.

**By sector.** Energy, real estate, information technology and so on. Spreading across sectors is what stops one sector's bad year from sinking the whole portfolio.

**By theme.** AI, growth, ESG, emerging markets, defensive, dividend, value. Yang explicitly says themes are not important for getting started; knowing which theme a holding belongs to is enough.

## Two ways to build a portfolio

The difference between the routes is really how much research time you are willing to spend.

**Individual stocks.** With \$1,000 split across five companies at \$200 each, one tripling lifts the whole portfolio; one going to zero or falling 50% makes you underperform the market in a volatile year. Yang's benchmark argument is blunt: the market return is the easiest benchmark to obtain, because an index fund simply delivers it. His cautionary example is Intel — a dot-com-era high-flyer whose 2000 peak had still not been surpassed at the time of recording, while the broad market had risen roughly four to five times over the same span. Stock picking can pay (he names Nvidia), but it requires research, conviction and tolerance for a volatile balance.

**Index funds.** One purchase of an S&P 500 ETF such as VOO spreads your money across hundreds of large US companies. The fund is passively managed — it buys what is in the index — so fees are very low and there is no manager to choose. Vanguard's mutual-fund version, VFIAX, tracks the same index. A ticker symbol is just an airport-style code for a tradable security: JFK for an airport, VOO for a fund. The trade-off is that you receive the market average, including its down years.

**Target-date funds.** A fund labelled with a retirement year (say 2060) starts heavily weighted in stocks and gradually shifts into bonds as that date approaches. These are most common inside 401(k) plans and are the "do nothing at all" option.

**The three-fund portfolio.** Yang's own allocation — shown in his M1 Finance app — is 60% US stocks (VTI, Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF), 30% international stocks (VEU, an ex-US ETF) and 10% bonds. The reasoning: if professional active managers struggle to beat the market, a DIY investor is better served by three low-cost funds that deliver diversification and market returns with an appropriate risk level. He is explicit that 60/30/10 is his split, not a universal answer; it depends on time horizon and risk tolerance. If you do choose individual stocks instead, he suggests a basket of 5–10 spread across sectors, because ten tech names all fall together when the sector does.

## Where you hold it: the account is a separate decision

You cannot buy a stock from a bank account. You need a brokerage account or a retirement account, and which one you choose determines the tax treatment of everything inside it.

**Taxable brokerage account.** The standard account. Yang lists Fidelity, M1 Finance, Charles Schwab, Robinhood, Vanguard, E\*TRADE, TD Ameritrade, Webull and Interactive Brokers, and personally recommends M1 Finance, Fidelity and Robinhood for beginners on the simple grounds that their interfaces are easy to learn.

**Roth IRA.** Contributions use after-tax dollars and qualified withdrawals are free of US federal income tax. The general IRA contribution limit in 2025 was \$7,000, with eligibility and the amount allowed subject to factors such as income and age. Yang’s hypothetical Apple gains illustrate the account distinction and assume a qualified withdrawal.

**401(k).** The video describes an employer-sponsored US retirement plan. Whether the employer matches contributions, and by how much, depends on the plan.

## Researching a company: five metrics and some soft signals

Yang splits investors into fundamental analysts, who estimate what a business is intrinsically worth from revenue, profit, cash flow, assets, liabilities and competitive position, and technical analysts, who trade chart patterns and short-term price action. This guide teaches the former; he considers it the more durable approach for beginners.

1. **Revenue** — total sales coming in.
2. **Net income** — profit left after all costs.
3. **P/E (price-to-earnings)** — how much you pay per dollar of earnings. A P/E of 20 means investors pay \$20 for each \$1 of annual profit. A high P/E usually prices in faster expected growth; a low one may mean undervaluation or a business in trouble.
4. **P/S (price-to-sales)** — share price divided by sales per share. Useful when a company is not yet profitable and P/E has no meaningful denominator; it must be used alongside other metrics.
5. **Free cash flow (FCF)** — commonly calculated as operating cash flow minus capital expenditures. Check the definition before comparing companies and interpret the measure in light of the business, especially for financial institutions.

The governing rule for P/E is **compare within the same sector or niche**. Tech companies grow faster than retailers and therefore carry higher P/E ratios as a group; a cross-sector comparison says nothing. His Yahoo Finance snapshots at the time of recording: Apple at roughly 38 times trailing-twelve-month earnings and Nvidia around 53, with the gap reflecting the growth the market had priced into Nvidia's AI chips.

Beyond the metrics, he describes his own 2024 position in Robinhood as an example of qualitative signals: the stock traded near \$15 in early 2024, sentiment was still soured by the GameStop era, yet Reddit users still treated it as their default broker, the company kept shipping features (a credit card, transfer bonuses), the founder was still running it, and its P/E was low relative to peers. He averaged in from February to August 2024 and says the position more than doubled. **This is his personal holding and his opinion, not a recommendation or a result anyone should expect to reproduce** — and he discloses that he posts his trades in a paid community.

## Placing the first order

Both walkthroughs are **US brokerage examples from the video**, recorded on Friday 17 January 2025 while the market was closed. Prices, screens, order rules and the trade window are those of that date and those two apps; other brokers and other markets behave differently.

**M1 Finance — portfolios as "pies".** A pie is a basket of slices, each slice a security with a target weight. The sequence matters, because creating a pie does not invest anything:

1. Open the account and transfer money from a bank; Yang says this generally takes one to two business days, and a new account starts at zero.
2. Go to **Invest → Build a stock and fund pie → Create a pie**, name it (here, "dividend portfolio") and add slices. A new pie needs at least one slice.
3. Rather than sorting by highest dividend yield — which he treats as a red flag — filter for a **0.5%–4%** yield, on the grounds that a company paying out too much has less to reinvest. Pick five: Microsoft, JPMorgan Chase, Exxon Mobil, Costco and Johnson & Johnson.
4. The five default to 20% each. He raises Microsoft to 25% and trims Johnson & Johnson to 15% so the pie still totals exactly 100% — the app enforces that constraint. Save.
5. **Add to portfolio.** The new pie now sits alongside the existing three-fund pie, which still holds 100% of the allocation. M1 requires the top-level pies to total 100%, so he sets the three-fund pie to 99% and the dividend pie to 1%, then Save → Confirm changes.
6. Click **Buy** and enter \$500. Because the market is closed, the order is queued for the next morning trade window — Tuesday in the recording, since Monday was a holiday — and the upcoming-activity screen shows the \$500 being split across the five stocks at their target weights.
7. Yang presents using new deposits to work toward the target weights as one reason he likes the setup. That is separate from placing the example buy order.

**Fidelity — order types and fractional shares.** Tap **Transact** (or the search icon), look up Coca-Cola, and open **Buy**. The quote shows \$62.11 as the last price; the order screen a moment later shows roughly \$62.71, which is the figure used in the fractional-share arithmetic below. On the order screen you choose:

- **Shares or dollars** — buy one whole share, or specify an amount such as \$100.
- **Market or limit** — a market order seeks prompt execution at the available price without guaranteeing that price. A buy limit order can execute at the limit or lower, and a sell limit order at the limit or higher; either may go unfilled. In this recording the market is closed, so the example market order is queued. Previewing the order does not submit it.
- **Fractional shares** — \$50 of Coca-Cola at \$62.71 buys about **0.797 shares**; \$50 of VOO at \$549.46 buys about **0.09 shares**. Fractional buying also works for ETFs.

A constraint he discovers while recording: **dollar-denominated orders for stocks and ETFs are only accepted during normal market hours**, while share-denominated orders can be queued over a weekend. His summary comparison: M1 is more rigid with individual stocks but easier to start with; Fidelity is more flexible but exposes more complexity, including options and margin trading.

## US taxes on investments

In a US taxable account, an increase in the share price generally becomes a realized capital gain when you sell. Dividends and fund capital gain distributions can be taxable while you still hold the investment.

- **Short-term capital gains.** Generally apply when the asset was held for one year or less, and are taxed at ordinary income rates. The example’s \$1,000 gain at an assumed 35% marginal rate gives \$350 of tax.
- **Long-term capital gains.** Generally apply to assets held for more than one year. The applicable US federal rate can be lower than the short-term rate and depends on taxable income and other conditions.
- **Reporting.** The brokerage may issue the relevant Form 1099. Check the transactions and cost basis before reporting them.
- **Tax-loss harvesting.** Realize losses to offset gains: \$10,000 of realized profit and a \$4,000 loss on a losing position (he uses GameStop as the example) leaves \$6,000 of taxable gain.
- **Roth IRA.** The tax treatment of investments held in a Roth IRA follows its distribution rules, including the requirements for a qualified withdrawal.

Yang discusses taxes as one part of an investing decision. For US federal rules, see the IRS pages on [capital gains](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409), [dividends](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc404) and [IRA distributions](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590b).

## What to take away

- **Motivation:** compare cash purchasing power and investment returns with attention to inflation, the measurement period and risk.
- **Arithmetic:** \$5,000 to start, \$1,000 a month, 8% assumed, 30 years → about \$1.54 million on \$365,000 of contributions, with growth concentrated in the final decade.
- **Language:** seven terms — bull/bear, market cap, index, froth, blue chip, penny stock, dividend.
- **Structure:** index funds are the default, individual stocks an optional sleeve, the three-fund portfolio the simplest multi-asset version.
- **Wrapper:** decide between a taxable brokerage and a Roth IRA before deciding what to buy.
- **Research:** revenue, net income, P/E, P/S, FCF — and P/E only against same-sector peers.
- **Discipline:** do not time the market, do not let taxes distort decisions, and do not mistake a low-looking price for a cheap company.

For readers outside the US, substitute local rules for the account and tax sections. Three habits survive the translation: work out what you can contribute every month before choosing a product; use a broad index fund as the core and cap individual stocks at an amount you could afford to lose; and write down your selling conditions in advance, so a downturn does not replace a plan with a reaction.

## Comprehension check

**1. Why is "not investing" itself a loss?**
Answer: When the rate earned on cash is below inflation, the balance can rise while purchasing power falls. Yang compares a hypothetical 0.1% savings rate with the historical stock index performance to illustrate opportunity cost, even though the two carry different risks.

**2. How does \$365,000 of contributions become \$1.5 million?**
Answer: Each month-end deposit compounds for the months remaining. The monthly deposit stream grows to about \$1.49 million, and the initial \$5,000 compounds for all 360 months to about \$54,679. Both use the same monthly rate of 8% ÷ 12.

**3. What does a P/E of 20 mean, and what can it not do?**
Answer: Investors are paying \$20 for each \$1 of the company's earnings, which embeds an expectation about future growth. It cannot compare companies across sectors: tech P/E ratios are structurally higher than retail ones, so the ratio only works as a same-sector, same-niche comparison.

**4. Why use P/S for a company that is not profitable?**
Answer: The P/E denominator is zero or negative, so the ratio is meaningless. Dividing price by sales per share still gives a valuation yardstick. It has to be read with other metrics, because revenue is not profit.

**5. Market or limit — what is the trade-off?**
Answer: A market order prioritizes execution and gives no price guarantee; a limit order prioritizes price and may never fill. On a thinly traded security, a market order can execute far from the quoted price.

**6. Why does holding more than a year reduce the tax bill?**
Answer: In a US taxable account, gains on assets held for more than one year generally qualify for long-term treatment and may be taxed at a lower rate than ordinary income. Dividends and fund capital gain distributions are taxed under their own rules.

**7. What can rolling US stock returns tell us about the safety of any entry point?**
Answer: The result describes a historical US stock sample, with dividends reinvested and inflation taken out. Change the market, sample window or holding period and the observed result can change. Even a 20- or 30-year horizon does not guarantee a positive return.

## Common traps

- **Reading "long-term upward" as "up every year"** — 2022 was down close to 18%; 8–10% is an average, not a promise.
- **Treating an index fund as loss-proof** — it removes single-stock risk, not market risk.
- **Comparing P/E across sectors** — tech and retail have different distributions by construction.
- **Refusing to sell to avoid tax** — profit after tax still beats no profit; tax matters at the margin, not as the strategy.
- **Chasing the highest dividend yield** — Yang deliberately filters to 0.5%–4%, because an unusually high yield often signals a company with too little left to reinvest.
